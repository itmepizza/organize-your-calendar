# Organize Your Calendar — The Three-Pillar Schedule Organization System

## When to use this skill

Use immediately when the user: wants to build a calendar/schedule; complains that their schedule always falls apart, they are often late, or they procrastinate; has previously given up on productivity apps or artistic Bullet Journals; wants to track habits but always abandons them; or simply says something like "I'm a lazy person, help me reorganize my life." Do not wait for the user to use the exact technical terms.

## Why this system differs from typical productivity apps

Most systems fail not because the user lacks discipline, but because the system is designed around an ideal scenario and leaves no room for real-world variables (Justin Sung). Willpower is a finite biological resource that is depleted over the course of the day — so the hardest task must be placed earliest. The lower the friction (one-tap digital notes), the easier it is to overload yourself with too much; moderate friction (handwriting) helps filter for what is truly worth doing. The three pillars below address exactly the three bottlenecks that make beginners quit after 2–3 days: a starting energy requirement that is too high, disguised perfectionism, and decision fatigue.

## The Three-Pillar Model

### Pillar 1 — Mindset: Dual Tolerance & Active Cutting

- **The time-doubling rule (2x buffer)**: estimate how long a task will take, then double that figure on the calendar. If the task finishes early, you have extra rest time; if it takes exactly as long as planned, the schedule still does not break. Never schedule things tightly.
- **Actively pick your loss**: before adding a new task, ask yourself "Is this truly necessary?" (Marcus Aurelius). Cut most of the far-fetched goals and keep only 1–2 mandatory tasks.
- **20-minute start**: lower the threshold for beginning — "just do exactly 20 minutes, then stopping is fine." Done is always better than perfect.

### Pillar 2 — Recording: The Low-Friction Analog Anchor Notebook

- One paper notebook + one pen, kept in a fixed spot on the desk, separate from the phone.
- Each month, draw exactly one double-page spread: date column | habit column | one "highlight" line.
- **One line per day**: in the evening before bed, write exactly one sentence summarizing the most memorable thing of the day. If you forget a day, fill it in later — no self-blame.
- Track a maximum of **2 small habits** over 30 days (never more than 2–3). The first week is always an observation week.
- **The Number 1 rule**: each morning, write at most 3 tasks and circle task number 1 — completing this one alone means the day is already a success; the other 2 are just bonus rewards.

### Pillar 3 — Time: The Four-Quarter Macro Calendar Frame

- Use a digital calendar (Google/Apple Calendar), but **do not subdivide beyond macro blocks**; at most 4 event blocks per day.
- **The four quarters of the day**: Morning (creative work / the hardest task) — Midday (rest/meal) — Afternoon (administrative work/light study) — Evening (relaxation/recovery). Losing one quarter does not mean the whole day is lost — the remaining quarters are still there.
- **Eat the frog first thing**: put the most important/hardest task in the morning focus block, when energy is at its highest.
- **3 survival tasks per day**: 1 work/study item, 1 health item, 1 relationship item. No more.

## 6-Week Transition Roadmap (do not force the whole system at once)

| Phase | Single goal | Actions |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1–2 | Recover and set up the analog anchor | Buy a notebook + pen; draw a minimalist monthly page; track only 1 easy habit in the evening; write 1 diary line each evening; practice the 20-minute rule |
| Weeks 3–4 | Shape the biological rhythm and macro blocks | Open the digital calendar and create at most 4 blocks/day (early morning – morning focus – afternoon work – evening relaxation); change the calendar colors to a soft, eye-friendly palette; apply the Number 1 rule every morning |
| Weeks 5–6 | Control tolerance and expand selectively | Apply the 2x buffer to every complex block; practice the reflex "four quarters, lose 1 and 3 remain"; add exactly 1 second quantitative metric (weight / pages read / hours slept) |

## Behavioral guidelines for Claude when applying this skill

- Always ask which week/phase the user is in (brand new, or already has a foundation) before making suggestions — do not pour the entire system in all at once.
- When creating a specific schedule/template for the user: at most 4 macro time blocks/day, at most 2 habits being tracked, always double the estimated time for complex tasks, always put the hardest task in the morning block.
- Use the term "action plan" instead of "task" when writing content for the user — this is a deliberate semantic friction that helps them feel more in control.
- If the user describes a packed schedule with no gaps: warn of the risk of a "domino collapse" when one item runs late, and suggest inserting a buffer right away.
- If the user demands a perfect schedule in 15-minute increments from the start: remind them of the principle "done is better than perfect" and pull them back to the correct weeks 1–2 phase first.
- If the user has maintained the routine steadily for ≥ 6 weeks and wants a more advanced system (3-tier Trident, multi-layer digitalization, etc.), consult `references/so-sanh-he-thong.md` to choose an expansion direction suited to their needs (freelancer, content creator, administrative employee, etc.).

## Output template when creating a daily plan for the user

```
### Today — [Weekday, day/month]

**Quarter 1 — Morning (Eat the frog)**
- [task number 1, estimated time already doubled]

**Quarter 2 — Midday**
- [rest / meal / light task]

**Quarter 3 — Afternoon**
- [administrative / light study task]

**Quarter 4 — Evening**
- [relaxation / prepare for sleep]

**3 Action Plans for today:**
1. (Number 1 — mandatory) ...
2. ...
3. ...

**Write in the notebook at the end of the day:** 1 highlight line + tick habits (max 2)
```

## Limitations

This is a framework synthesized from popular practice methods (Ryder Carroll, Reysu, Nathaniel Drew, Ali Abdaal, Julia Broome, Miles Mochizuki, Justin Sung...), not the result of randomized controlled research — treat it as a reference tool, not an absolute law. For severe chronic procrastination, clinical ADHD, or depression, this skill is not a substitute for professional medical/psychological consultation.
